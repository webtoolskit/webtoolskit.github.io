Percentage, Grades and Class in Indian Colleges: What Your Marks Really Mean

By Leena JadhavPublished 14 November 20268 min read

Your marksheet says 8.2 CGPA, a form asks for a percentage, and an interviewer asks if you passed with first class. This guide explains how those three things connect, and why the answer changes from one university to the next.

How to calculate a percentage

Add up the marks you scored, add up the maximum marks, divide the first by the second, and multiply by 100.

FormulaPercentage = (total marks scored ÷ total maximum marks) × 100

Say you sat six papers of 100 marks each and scored 78, 85, 64, 71, 90 and 69. The total is 457 out of 600, so your percentage is 457 ÷ 600 × 100 = 76.17%.

Be careful when the papers have different maximums. If you scored 45 out of 50 in one paper (90%) and 60 out of 100 in another (60%), the average of the two percentages is 75%. The correct figure is 105 out of 150, which is 70%. Always add the marks first and divide once.

What first class and distinction mean

Most Indian universities sort results into four steps: pass class, second class, first class, and first class with distinction. Each university decides the percentage where a step begins, and the numbers are not the same everywhere.

The table shows how far apart real rules can be. These are examples from two universities, not a complete list.

Take a student with 75%. At MSU Baroda that is first class with distinction in every faculty. At Kristu Jayanti it is first class in an undergraduate course and distinction in a postgraduate one.

So the figures you may have heard, 60% for first class and 75% for distinction, are common but not universal. Your own university's ordinance is the only source that counts.

One more point for school students. The CBSE FAQ says the Board “does not have provision of awarding overall division/distinction/aggregate”, so a CBSE marksheet will not say first class or distinction on its own.

Grades and grade points

Many colleges use a 10-point grading scale. Each paper gets a letter grade, each letter carries a grade point, and a weighted average of the grade points is your SGPA for a semester and your CGPA across the course.

The trouble is that the letters are not standard. Both rules below give a 65% paper a grade point of 7, yet the letter on the marksheet differs.

Even inside one university, the scale can change between batches. Kristu Jayanti's table for the 2024 undergraduate batch labels 50–59% as B with grade point 6. For the 2025 and 2026 batches, the same band is labelled B+ with grade point 6, and B moves down to 40–49.99%.

The band edges differ too. Kristu Jayanti's A grade runs from 60.00 to 69.99%, while MSU Baroda's grade point 7 is printed as 60.1 to 70.0%. If you are close to an edge, read your own table.

Converting CGPA to percentage

CGPA is a weighted average of grade points, so it has to be converted to a percentage before it can be compared with a marks-based result. There is no national formula, and universities have chosen different ones.

CBSE published an “indicative” rule in Circular No. 24 of 28 May 2010, written for admission to Class XI after Class X grading: overall indicative percentage = 9.5 × CGPA. The CBSE FAQ repeats it for Class X. I found nothing in either document that applies it to a college CGPA.

Kristu Jayanti says on its grading page: “Percentage equivalent of CGPA = CGPA * 10”, with 8.95 equal to 89.50%.

MSU Baroda's ordinance asks for the equivalent percentage to be worked out from the CGPA with one decimal place, but it does not give a multiplier.

The gap matters. A CGPA of 8.4 is 84.0% at ×10 and 79.8% at ×9.5, a difference of 4.2 points. The gap is half of your CGPA in percentage points, 3.0 at a CGPA of 6 and 5.0 at a CGPA of 10, so it grows as the CGPA rises.

Here is how that can change a result. Suppose a university sets distinction at 75%. A CGPA of 7.6 gives 76.0% at ×10, which clears the line, but 72.2% at ×9.5, which does not. Same student, same CGPA, different class.

Where to find your university's rule

Look for three things, in this order.

  • The grading table. It is usually printed on the back of the marksheet or in the examination regulations. It shows letters, grade points and mark bands for your batch.
  • The class or division rule. This is normally in the university ordinance or the student handbook, under results or award of class.
  • The conversion rule. If your university has a CGPA to percentage formula, it will be published by the examination office. If it has none, ask the office what percentage figure the college or employer you are dealing with will accept.

Ask for the version for your own batch. Rules change, as the Kristu Jayanti example shows.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming 75% is distinction everywhere. The examples above start it at 66%, 70%, 75% and 80%.
  • Using a random website's formula. Many sites repeat one multiplier for every university. Use the one your university publishes.
  • Averaging percentages. When papers have different maximums, add the marks first and divide once.
  • Rounding up yourself. A figure such as 74.96% is not 75%. Follow your university's rounding rule, which can be one decimal or two.
  • Using an older batch's table. Grade letters and bands can change between batches.
