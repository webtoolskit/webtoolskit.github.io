The 75% Attendance Rule in Indian Colleges: How It Works and What to Do If You Fall Short

By Leena JadhavPublished 14 November 20268 min read

If you have ever stared at your attendance sheet wondering whether you are safe, this guide is for you. It explains how the 75% rule works, how to work out your own number, how many classes you can still skip, and what to do if you fall short.

What the 75% rule is

Most Indian colleges want you in class for at least 75% of the sessions before they let you sit the semester exam. That number is common, but the details change from one university to the next.

The figure is usually traced to the UGC's 2003 regulations on minimum standards of instruction, which a law firm's summary quotes as 75% of lectures, tutorials, seminars and practical sessions. Your own university's ordinance is what actually applies to you, so read that before you trust any blog, including this one.

The part that catches people out is how attendance is counted. Some colleges look at your total across all subjects. Others look at each subject on its own, and many keep a higher bar for labs.

WhereHow the rule is written
Gujarat University (as summarised here)75% overall and 70% in each subject
An SPPU-affiliated college (MMCC exam policy)75% in each subject
Rajagiri School of Engineering & Technology (condonation rules)75% of the total duration of the course

If your college counts per subject, one weak subject can stop you from writing the exam even when your overall figure looks fine.

How to calculate your attendance

Divide the classes you attended by the classes actually held, then multiply by 100.

FormulaAttendance % = (classes attended ÷ classes held) × 100

Say your college has held 60 classes in a subject so far and you were there for 42. That is 42 ÷ 60 × 100 = 70%. You are five points under the line.

Two things trip people up here. Use classes held, not classes scheduled for the whole semester, because the total keeps growing every week. And do the sum for each subject separately, even if your college also shows an overall figure.

How many classes you can miss, or must attend

If you are below 75%, you need to attend some number of classes in a row, with no absences, to climb back to the line. If you are above it, you can afford to skip a few.

Both answers come from the same idea: each class you attend raises attended and held together, and each class you skip raises only held.

Classes to attend in a row3 × held − 4 × attended
Classes you can skip(4 × attended ÷ 3) − held

Take the student from the last section: 42 attended out of 60 held. Three times 60 minus four times 42 is 180 − 168 = 12. If they attend the next 12 classes, they will have attended 54 of 72, which is exactly 75%.

Now a student with 48 attended out of 60 held (80%). Four times 48 divided by 3 is 64, minus 60 gives 4. They can skip 4 classes in a row and still sit at exactly 75% (48 of 64). If 20 more classes are scheduled, they must attend at least 12 of them and can miss up to 8.

Sitting exactly on 75% leaves no room for a sick day. Most people are safer keeping a cushion of a few points.

If you fall below 75%

Falling short is not always the end of the road, because many colleges allow “condonation”: the college forgives a limited shortage when you have a valid reason and the paperwork to prove it.

The limits are set by your university or college, not by a national rule. Two examples show how different they can be.

Rajagiri School of Engineering & TechnologyKerala University
Who can applyA student with a shortage of up to 10%, on genuine medical groundsA student who could not keep 75% for reasons beyond their control
DeadlineAt least 10 days before the semester examAt least 10 days before the university exam
Who decidesThe Principal, with the course teacher and the HoDThe Principal must recommend the application
Extra conditionsAllowed once in a 2-year course and twice in longer coursesAbsence of more than five days in a row needs a medical certificate if the reason is medical, and you must list each day missed with a reason

Sources for the table: Rajagiri and the Kerala University guidelines, which are undated, so check the current version.

Other colleges accept reasons beyond illness. A 2026 feature on the 75% rule lists NCC duty, NSS work, university sports, placement drives and fieldwork. It says proof should be on official letterhead with a seal, the exact dates and contact details, and that a request usually needs a covering letter, your attendance printout, the course codes and the proofs.

If you miss the cut, your hall ticket may be delayed or refused. Some colleges issue a provisional one while your condonation is pending. If it is withheld, the same feature suggests writing to your HoD first, then the Dean, then the Controller of Examinations.

As for the rumour that 74.9% means you automatically lose the year, the same feature says that is not automatic everywhere. Check your own college's rule before you panic.

Mistakes that cost students their exam eligibility

Most attendance trouble comes from a handful of avoidable slips.

  • Checking only the overall number. If your college counts each subject separately, a 55% in one subject can block you even with a healthy average.
  • Leaving it until exam week. Both condonation rules above ask for the application at least 10 days before the exams start. After that, it may not be considered.
  • Sending weak proof. A doctor's note with no dates or a missing seal can be rejected. Ask for exact dates, a seal and contact details before you leave the clinic.
  • Treating condonation as a right. At Rajagiri it is allowed once in a 2-year course, and the Principal decides each case on its merits.
  • Trusting an old rule. Ordinances change. Look at this year's handbook or notice, not a senior's memory of it.
